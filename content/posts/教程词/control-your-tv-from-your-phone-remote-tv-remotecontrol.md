## Background

There are really two different ways to control a TV from a phone, and Reddit conversations often blur them together.

The first is local network control. This is what most people mean when they use an official Roku, Android TV, or Google TV phone remote. In those cases, the phone and TV talk over Wi-Fi. That is why users in Roku discussions keep repeating the same fix: make sure the phone and the TV are on the same network, and if discovery fails, try reconnecting or even entering the device IP directly. In one older Roku thread, the practical advice was exactly that: same network first, manual IP as a fallback if the TV does not appear in the app.

The second method is infrared, or IR. A few Reddit threads about universal remote apps and phone hardware point out that some phones still include an IR blaster, while many do not. If your phone has one, it can behave more like a traditional universal remote and does not depend on the TV being on your Wi-Fi. But community comments also make it clear that IR on phones is less common now, and setup quality varies a lot by device and app.

That distinction matters because it explains why one person can say “my phone remote works perfectly” while another says “the app never finds my TV.” They may not even be using the same kind of control.

## Why this matters

The obvious reason is convenience. A physical remote disappears into a couch, runs out of battery, or stops pairing at the worst possible time. But the Reddit angle shows something more specific: phone remotes solve a few pain points better than many original remotes do.

Typing is the biggest one. In remote-app discussions for Android TV and Google TV, users repeatedly highlight phone keyboard input as one of the most useful features. That tracks with real life. Entering a password, searching YouTube, or signing into a streaming app is slow with arrow keys and an on-screen alphabet. A phone keyboard is simply faster.

The second is recovery. In a Roku thread from 2026, one user described relying on the mobile app for years after losing the physical remote, only to run into a connection problem later. The details of that thread are less important than the use case itself: the phone remote had become the primary remote, not a backup. That is common enough that when the app fails, the problem feels urgent.

The third is feature overlap. Some Reddit posts about Android TV remote apps mention extras such as touchpad navigation, voice input, or automatic reconnect. Even when people complain about cluttered or ad-heavy apps, those are the features they still want. So if your goal is to control your TV from your phone, the best setup is not just “anything that connects.” It is the one that gives you dependable basics plus the one or two features you will actually use.

## What to pay attention to

Start with the simplest checkpoint: are the phone and TV on the same Wi-Fi network? That advice comes up so often on Reddit because it really is the first failure point. If the TV is on a guest network, a different band, or a separate router, device discovery can fail even when both devices technically have internet access.

Next, use the official app or the built-in remote feature for your platform before chasing third-party universal remote apps. Reddit threads about Android TV and Roku both include frustration with unreliable detection or missing features in some apps, while users also note that official tools tend to be the baseline worth testing first. If the official option does not work, that tells you something useful about the device or network setup.

After that, check whether the TV-side service is current. In the 2022 Android TV thread, the fix that solved the problem for multiple users was updating the Android TV remote service on the TV itself. That is an easy detail to miss because people often update the phone app and assume the TV end is fine.

It is also worth deciding whether you need Wi-Fi control or IR control. If your phone does not have an IR blaster, no universal remote app can magically create one. Reddit users discussing IR blasters make this point indirectly: some phones still have the hardware, many do not, and people often discover that too late. If your phone lacks IR, you are relying on network-based control.

Finally, keep expectations realistic. Reddit comments about IR and phone remotes regularly mention one tradeoff that sounds minor until you feel it: using a phone is not always as frictionless as grabbing a dedicated remote. You unlock the phone, open the app, wait for a connection, then tap a glass screen with no tactile buttons. A phone remote is excellent as a backup and often better for typing, but it is not automatically better at everything.

## FAQ

### Can I control any TV from my phone?

Not every TV in the same way. Based on the Reddit discussions, smart TVs and streaming platforms such as Roku, Android TV, and Google TV are the most likely to support phone control over Wi-Fi. Older or simpler TVs may need IR, which only works if your phone actually has an IR blaster.

### Why does my phone remote app not find my TV?

The most common Reddit answer is the least glamorous one: the phone and TV are not really on the same network, or the TV-side remote service needs attention. On Roku threads, users also suggest trying the official app and, if discovery fails, entering the device IP directly when the app allows it. On Android TV threads, updating the remote service on the TV was a reported fix for some users.

### Is a phone remote better than the original remote?

Usually it is better at specific tasks, not better at everything. Reddit users consistently praise phone keyboards for search and login screens, and some like voice input or touchpad controls. But they also complain that phones are slower to unlock, launch, and aim than a physical remote with dedicated buttons.

### Do I need a special app?

Often yes, but the “special” part depends on the platform. The Reddit pattern is to start with the official or platform-native option for Roku, Android TV, or Google TV first. Third-party apps exist, but community comments often describe them as inconsistent, ad-heavy, or locked behind paywalls.
