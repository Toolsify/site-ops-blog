The Hacker News thread [“Ask HN: How do you deal with people who trust LLMs?”](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=47433702) is not really a debate about whether AI is useful. It is a debate about what happens when people stop treating large language models as tools and start treating them as authorities.

That distinction matters. In the thread, the original poster describes a familiar situation: someone asks a chatbot a factual question, gets a polished answer, and accepts it as objective truth instead of checking a reputable source. The replies do not produce one neat consensus, but they do surface a practical pattern. Most people are not worried about LLMs because they are new. They are worried because LLMs can sound confident while hiding where the answer came from, and that makes bad information easier to repeat.

If you landed here searching for *ask hn how do you deal with people who trust llms explained*, the most useful reading is not “never use AI” and not “trust the model more.” It is learning how the thread frames trust, accountability, and verification.

## Background

The Ask HN post starts with a simple question: what do you do when people use LLMs as a source of truth? That framing set off a long discussion, and the split was revealing.

One group argued that LLMs are just the newest version of an old problem. People have always trusted weak sources, repeated rumors, or quoted whatever sounded persuasive. From that angle, AI is not uniquely dangerous; it is another place where people still need judgment. One commenter put the burden on the person, not the tool: if someone presents information, they are responsible for whether it is right.

Another group pushed back and said LLMs are different in one important way: they compress and restate information without making the source obvious. A bad website, a shaky article, or a biased broadcast can still be inspected as a source. A chatbot answer often arrives as a clean summary, which means the user can skip the habit of asking, “Who is saying this, and why should I believe it?”

That tension drives the whole thread. The discussion is not really “humans good, AI bad.” It is about whether removing friction from information retrieval also removes the healthy friction of source-checking.

## Why this matters

This topic matters because trust habits do not stay inside casual chat. They spill into work, school, hiring, coding, research, customer support, and day-to-day decision-making. That is why *ai trust in professional settings* is a more serious question than it first appears.

Several comments in the thread point to the same practical issue: LLM outputs are persuasive because they are coherent. They often look finished before they are verified. In older search behavior, a user had to click, skim, compare, and interpret. That process was imperfect, but it forced at least some contact with the underlying source. With LLMs, the answer can arrive already flattened into a plausible paragraph.

The thread also shows why this becomes socially tricky. If you tell someone “that’s wrong because chatbots hallucinate,” they may hear that as a broad anti-AI position. If you say nothing, you allow a weak standard of evidence to stand. In professional environments, neither extreme works well. You need a way to challenge the claim without turning every conversation into a culture war about technology.

That is the real value of the Ask HN discussion. It moves the issue away from hype and toward standards: what counts as evidence, what level of verification is expected, and who owns the risk when an AI-generated answer is wrong.

## What to pay attention to

A few recurring points in the thread are worth watching closely.

First, accountability comes up again and again. One strong line of argument is that people should be held to the same standard whether they used a chatbot, a bad blog post, or their own memory. That is a useful standard because it avoids treating AI as magical. If someone makes a claim that affects a decision, they should be able to support it.

Second, source visibility matters. Some commenters argued that all sources carry bias, which is true in a broad sense. But others answered that editorial processes, peer review, reputation, and traceable authorship still create a meaningful difference between a named source and a model output. Even when a newspaper or paper is flawed, you can inspect who wrote it, where it was published, and what incentives shaped it. A chatbot answer often obscures all of that.

Third, the thread highlights a subtle problem: many people do not “blindly trust” LLMs in a naive way. They trust them because the answer sounds complete and there is no obvious warning signal. That is a more important failure mode than simple gullibility. The form of the answer creates false confidence.

So when reading *ask hn how do you deal with people who trust llms*, pay attention to how often the discussion returns to standards and process rather than model branding.

## ask hn how do you deal with people who trust llms guide

If you want an actual *ask hn how do you deal with people who trust llms guide*, the thread suggests a practical approach.

Start by challenging the claim, not the person. Instead of saying “you should not trust AI,” ask, “What source is that based on?” or “Can we verify that in the original document?” This keeps the conversation grounded in evidence. It also avoids a moralizing tone, which usually makes people defensive.

Next, ask for provenance. One commenter suggested treating the other person as if they had real expertise and then asking which book, expert, or non-AI source supports the point. That is a strong move because it tests whether the AI answer can be traced to something inspectable. If the source cannot be named, that is useful information by itself.

Then, separate low-stakes and high-stakes use. For brainstorming, summarizing, drafting, or generating search directions, the thread leaves room for LLMs as complementary tools. But when the answer affects a concrete decision, the standard should rise. The more the output matters, the less acceptable “the model said so” becomes.

It also helps to demonstrate the problem instead of lecturing about it. One commenter recommended pushing the model, contradicting it, and watching how readily it flips into agreement. That is not scientific proof, but it can quickly show why confidence and correctness are different things. In many cases, a short live example teaches more than a long warning about hallucinations.

Finally, protect your own baseline. The thread includes a mental-health angle that is easy to overlook. You do not need to correct every bad AI-mediated claim in every setting. If the stakes are low, it may be enough to model better habits rather than police every conversation. In other words, dealing with LLM trust explained in the most practical way means choosing where verification actually matters.

## FAQ

## Is the Ask HN thread arguing that LLMs are useless?

No. The discussion is more specific than that. Many commenters accept that LLMs are useful for drafting, summarizing, or exploring a topic. The concern is what happens when users treat the output itself as verified knowledge instead of a starting point.

## How should I use ask hn how do you deal with people who trust llms in real life?

Use it as a guide to conversation, not as a slogan. The most practical move is to ask for the underlying source, especially when the claim affects work, money, health, or other people. That shifts the discussion from tool preference to evidence quality.

## Are LLMs really different from search engines or Wikipedia?

The thread suggests yes and no. No, because people have always trusted weak sources. Yes, because LLMs often remove visible provenance and present a polished answer that feels settled. That presentation layer is a meaningful difference.

## What is the biggest risk in professional settings?

The biggest risk is not just hallucination. It is unearned confidence. In meetings or workflows, an answer that sounds authoritative can move faster than anyone’s willingness to verify it. That is why *ai trust in professional settings* is mostly a process problem.

## What to watch next

This Ask HN thread is likely to stay relevant because it captures a transition moment. More people now interact with knowledge through chat interfaces, not search results. That shift will keep raising the same questions: when is AI a convenience layer, when is it a liability, and what proof should be required before an answer influences a real decision?

The next thing to watch is not whether arguments about hallucinations continue. They will. The bigger question is whether teams, schools, and communities build clearer norms around source disclosure, verification, and accountability. If they do, LLMs can remain useful without becoming default authorities. If they do not, the same polished uncertainty will keep spreading under a different interface.

## Final takeaway

The best reading of *ask hn how do you deal with people who trust llms* is not “reject AI” and not “trust it more carefully.” It is simpler: require evidence at the point where evidence matters. The Hacker News thread keeps circling back to the same principle. LLMs may be helpful, fast, and often impressive, but they do not remove the need to ask where a claim came from, who stands behind it, and how much it matters if the answer is wrong.
